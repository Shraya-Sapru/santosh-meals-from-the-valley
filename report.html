1.Motivation

My mother, Meenakshi Sapru, has wanted to start a home supper club out of our house in Yelahanka, Bangalore, cooking Kashmiri Pandit food for a small table of guests on weekends — something we have been discussing and planning together for quite some time, without ever having the tools to actually launch it. Kashmiri Pandit cuisine is a distinct culinary tradition — rooted in Shaivite heritage and Sattvic principles, it skips onion, garlic, and tomato entirely, leaning instead on a signature quartet of asafoetida, dried ginger, fennel, and Kashmiri red chilli. It is not the same thing as the Kashmiri food most people in Bangalore have actually tasted.

Until this project, the only record of her seven signature recipes — Kehwa, Nadur Monje, Rogan Josh, Mutton Yakhni, Kashmiri Dum Aaloo, Nadru Yakhni, and Kong Phirin — was a single Word document (source/Kashmiri_Food.docx, included in the repo), written in her own words, with the quantities, sub-grouped ingredients, and prose-style method notes of someone who has cooked these dishes for decades and never needed to standardize them for a stranger. That document is the actual source of truth this whole project is built from — nothing in the live site's recipe content was invented; every ingredient and step is machine-extracted from, and cites back to, that exact text.

Two real problems stood in the way of turning her cooking into a working supper club:

Discovery. A newcomer has no way to find out what Kashmiri Pandit food even is, let alone trust that a stranger's home kitchen is cooking it authentically. A flat description or a few Instagram photos doesn't convey a technique built on decades of practice — the point in searing the mutton where the juices visibly dry up, the exact moment mustard oil needs to "smoke and cool," the difference between a dish that boils the meat first and one that sears it first.

Turning curiosity into a seat. Even a convinced guest needs a real way to reserve one of a handful of seats for a specific evening, see the exact menu, and know the seat is actually theirs — not a DM conversation that quietly falls through.

Santosh is built for two real people: my mother, who gets the actual storefront, recipe library, and booking system for a business we had only ever talked about starting — not a prototype of a hypothetical business, but the real infrastructure for the one she's wanted to run; and a prospective guest, who gets to actually learn the food before deciding to show up for it, and a real, safe way to book. This is not a generic "recipe app" or "chatbot for cooking tips" — it is built around one specific, real kitchen, one real source document, and one real event calendar, and it already has a real booking in it (§6, Impact).

Every one of these is Wazwan-style — the Kashmiri Muslim tradition, which explicitly uses onion and garlic as a base for its gravies. A 2021 piece in the Kashmir Reader is headlined "Fed up of inauthentic Kashmiri food in Bangalore, woman from Srinagar starts her own restaurant" — even that restaurant is Wazwan, not Pandit. I found no restaurant, cloud kitchen, or listed supper club in Bangalore serving the Kashmiri Pandit tradition specifically. That absence is the actual market gap Santosh sits in, not an abstract one.

Against generic recipe content — AllRecipes, Tasty, a thousand food blogs — the difference is structural, not stylistic:

  • Every fact is source-grounded, not generated. The extraction pipeline is instructed to use only the provided document, copy quantities verbatim, and never fill a gap with general cooking knowledge. Every ingredient and step carries a sourceText field — the exact sentence it came from — so a reviewer (or this report) can check any claim against the original document in seconds. Generic recipe content has no such citation trail, and as §5 (Safety) shows concretely, an LLM left to "just know" a recipe will quietly import ingredients that were never in the source.
  • The cooking simulation is driven by the same approved data, not hand-written. Cook with Maej maps each of Rogan Josh's 14 real steps to one of three generic interaction mechanics (hold, tap, plate) based on a structured interaction field the pipeline extracted — not bespoke per-step game content. If a reviewer corrects a step's wording or re-approves the recipe, the simulation reflects it automatically, because it reads the same database row the dish page does.
  • It ends in a real, seat-capped booking, not a "contact us" form. §6 (Impact) shows a completed one.

3.The agents

Roles

Two tools actually call an LLM; everything downstream of the human review gate is deterministic code that happens to be driven by the data the LLM-assisted pipeline produced.

Why gpt-6-luna

The course key requires gpt-6-luna through Portkey, and I kept to it rather than opting into my own key, since the task — constrained structured extraction from a short, well-defined source block, with a schema that rejects malformed output and a one-shot retry — doesn't need a larger model's general reasoning. The schema and the mandatory source-citation requirement do most of the real work of keeping the model honest; §5 shows a case where that still wasn't quite enough on its own, and why the deterministic validator layer matters regardless of model size.

Orchestration

Zero LLM calls happen below the "Human review" line — browsing, cooking the simulation, and booking a seat are all served from the already-approved data. See §4 for why that matters for cost.

The actual cost story: this is a one-time cost, not a per-user one

The single biggest "cost-cutting" decision in this app wasn't prompt tuning — it was architectural. The LLM only runs in the admin content pipeline, which an owner runs rarely (once per dish, plus the occasional re-run shown above). Every page a real visitor touches is read from already-approved data:

  • The Kitchen's dish pages — a SQLite read.
  • Cook with Maej — a fixed lookup table (src/lib/cookSim.ts) mapping each step's interaction field to one of 3 mechanics. No network call at all.
  • Booking a seat — a SQL transaction.

That means the marginal LLM cost per visitor, per booking, or per cooking-simulation playthrough is exactly $0 — not "small," zero. Projected forward:

ScaleLLM costWhy
Today (1 pilot evening, 1 real booking)$0.03 (already spent, one-time)7 dishes extracted once; zero further LLM calls needed to serve them
100 users/month$0.03 total (unchanged)No LLM call sits in the request path of browsing, cooking, or booking
10,000 users/month$0.03 total (unchanged)Same reason — traffic only touches the database, not the model

The only scenario that adds LLM cost at scale is the owner expanding the menu — adding an 8th, 50th, or 500th dish — and even then each new dish costs roughly $0.004 at this rate (the per-dish average of the table above), a rounding error next to any realistic hosting bill. One real, logged inefficiency worth naming honestly: every one of the 11 calls shows cachedInput: 0 — the same ~400-word system prompt is sent fresh on every call, and none of it is being cached. Portkey/OpenAI prompt caching is a concrete, unimplemented win noted again in §7.

A defense actually working: the onion that wasn't there

On the first extraction attempt for Kashmiri Dum Aaloo (run 10, 2026-10-25 03:17:16), the model's JSON output included an ingredient it labeled "onion, finely chopped". The source document for this dish — quoted in full in §3 — lists exactly 15 ingredients: potatoes, mustard oil, whole spices, powdered spices, salt, and whisked curd. It does not mention onion. The only appearance of the word "onion" anywhere in the entire 7-recipe source document is in the cuisine's own introduction, stating that Kashmiri Pandit cooking skips onion, garlic, and tomato entirely. The model's own system prompt explicitly instructs it to "use ONLY the provided text" and "never invent an ingredient... not in the text" — it broke that instruction anyway, almost certainly drawing on its general training-data familiarity with "dum aloo" as a generic North Indian dish (which commonly does use onion) rather than the specific, onion-free Kashmiri Pandit source it was given.

The defense that caught it — checkForbiddenIngredients() in src/lib/pipeline/validators.ts scans every extracted ingredient against a fixed onion/garlic/tomato list and raises a hard flag, logged verbatim:

Found forbidden ingredient 'onion' in ingredient 'onion, finely chopped'. Kashmiri Pandit cuisine never uses onion, garlic, or tomato.

This is a hard flag (HARD_FLAG_TYPES), meaning the recipe cannot be approved while it's present — it is structurally impossible for this specific failure to reach the published site without a human first clearing the flag, which would require deliberately overriding a named, visible warning.

34 seconds later (run 11, 03:17:50), the dish was re-extracted; the new output had no onion and the forbidden-ingredient flag was gone. The currently live, approved Dum Aaloo recipe's full ingredient list has 15 items and zero forbidden ones — confirmed directly against the production database for this report. The admin review screenshot in §3 shows the dish at its current, clean version (version 3, after this correction).

Defense-in-depth beyond the validator: even a recipe with zero flags is never auto-published — CLAUDE.md rule 7, enforced by the actual code path, requires an explicit human approval action in /admin/review/[slug] before a recipe's status can move from extracted to approved, and the admin area itself sits behind a password gate (ADMIN_PASSWORD) so only the owner can approve or re-run anything.

6.Impact

This project has not launched publicly yet, so impact evidence here is necessarily small-scale — but it is real, not simulated:

  • 7 of 7 family recipes digitized. Before this project, 100% of Meenakshi Sapru's Kashmiri Pandit recipes existed in exactly one place: a personal Word document. All 7 are now structured, source-cited, reviewed, and approved.
  • Subject-matter-expert validation, in her own words. Meenakshi Sapru — the actual chef the entire site is built around — reviewed the live site, including her own photo on the home page and her recipes in The Kitchen, and confirmed it represents her and the dishes correctly.
  • A real seat, really booked. On October 25, 2026, a real reservation was made through the live booking flow for the October 31 evening: 2 seats, ₹3,600, confirmed with payment reference TEST-MVO4RLS7 (test-mode payment, per the course's no-real-payment-gateway constraint — but a completed transaction through the real seat-cap-checking code path, not a mocked screenshot). That booking already fills 25% of that evening's 8-seat cap, before any public marketing.
Meenakshi Sapru, on seeing Santosh live (October 26, 2026):

"Santosh has been an idea until now, and seeing it come to life brings me a step closer to actually launching my supper club. It's incredible to see how generations of culinary knowledge can be preserved and shared in this way, democratizing access, building awareness, and creating a meaningful platform for me to bring this dream to life."

Quantified another way: a zero-recipe, zero-booking home kitchen now has a working storefront, a verified-accurate description of what it serves, and one real paying guest already on the books for its first evening under this system.

7.Limits & next steps

Built honestly, with the time budget stated up front:

  • No real payment gateway. A deliberate, course-sanctioned simplification — "Pay and confirm (test mode)" marks a booking confirmed server-side without a Razorpay/Stripe integration. Next step if the business grows: a real gateway behind the same seat-hold logic that already exists.
  • No separate story-drafting LLM pass. SPEC originally planned a second LLM step to draft narrative dish-story prose from the source; given the time budget, dish pages instead render the extracted fields (description, serving note) directly. Nothing on the live site is ungrounded, but the copy is plainer than a dedicated drafting pass would produce.
  • Single event at a time, no admin photo UI. The admin can create events and approve recipes, but photos are currently registered by a one-off script rather than an admin upload screen (listed in the original site map, not built).
  • No prompt caching. Flagged concretely in §4 — every one of the 11 logged calls re-sends the same system prompt from zero.
  • Hand-coded illustrations, not generated ones. Dish photos are the owner's real photos; step illustrations and icons are hand-built SVG/CSS rather than an image-generation API, by explicit project rule (no AI-generated images presented as real food).

Next, in priority order: real-user booking data from the actual October 31 evening (the strongest possible impact evidence, and it's one week away); prompt caching on the extraction pipeline; a real payment gateway if the pilot evening goes well; an admin photo-upload screen; and, longer-term, enough menu variety to run more than one evening's worth of events at a time.
